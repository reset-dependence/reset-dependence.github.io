Reset Dependence Is Embodied: Training Protocol Changes Which Morphologies Appear Optimal

1 University of Warsaw 2 Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam

Changing only the reset rule used to train controllers changes which of 100 evolved robot bodies look best, and how much a body loses without fall resets differs across evolutionary families.

In short

  • The reset rule reorders the bodies. After Strict and Reset-free training, the rankings of all 100 bodies correlate at ρ = 0.21, against about 0.89 if seed noise were the only difference.
  • Removing fall resets costs flat-ground bodies the most. Bodies evolved on flat ground lose 45% of their per-step reward, bodies evolved on variable terrain only 16%.
  • Robust selection costs little. Bodies chosen by their worst reward across the three rules keep at least 91% of each rule’s best reward in sample, and over 80% averaged across held-out seeds.

What we tested

We train PPO controllers from scratch for 100 bodies of the UNIMAL design space, evolved by DERL on flat ground (Floor), on variable terrain (VT) and for pushing a box (MVT), all on one task: walking forward on flat ground. During training, an episode ends when the torso falls below a fraction τ of its starting height: Standard (τ = 0.20), Strict (τ = 0.50) or Reset-free (no fall cutoff; the 1,000-step time limit stays). With three seeds per rule that makes 900 controllers, and one common evaluator scores them all. The results also hold without the bodies whose cutoffs do not act as intended (paper, Section 3).

Families respond differently

The difference holds in permutation tests over bodies and without an evaluation cutoff, and on 24 bodies, Floor bodies gain the most from Strict over Reset-free training under both PPO and SAC (paper, Sections 4.4 to 4.6).

Some bodies walk under every rule

An audit for co-design benchmarks

  1. State the reset rule and check that it acts on every body. Compare each body’s cutoff with its resting torso height and with the drop from its reset pose.
  2. Retrain the top bodies under one looser and one stricter threshold, and score every controller with a common evaluator (120 runs for 20 bodies and three seeds).
  3. Compare rank agreement with seed noise, and report bodies that stay strong under every rule (paper, Section 5).

Abstract

Robot co-design benchmarks rank bodies after training their controllers under a fixed episode protocol, but that protocol can change the ranking itself. Consequently, two studies can select different bodies from the same corpus even when they use the same task and evaluator. We study one part of that protocol, the reset rule, which ends a training episode when the robot’s torso falls below a height threshold. We train PPO controllers for 100 DERL-evolved UNIMAL bodies with this threshold at 20% of each body’s starting torso height (Standard), at 50% (Strict), or with no threshold (Reset-free), and score every controller with the same evaluator. The threshold changes which bodies look best: the rankings after Strict and Reset-free training correlate at only 0.21, against about 0.89 if seed noise were the only difference. Removing fall resets costs some bodies far more than others. Bodies evolved on flat ground lose 45% of their per-step reward relative to Standard training, and those evolved on variable terrain only 16%. Permutation tests over bodies support this difference, and on 24 bodies, flat-ground bodies gain the most from Strict over Reset-free training under both PPO and SAC. Choosing bodies by their worst reward across the three protocols costs little: in sample, they keep at least 91% of the reward of each protocol’s best bodies, and over 80% averaged across held-out seeds. Co-design benchmarks should state their reset rule, test whether their rankings depend on it, and report bodies that stay strong across protocols.
